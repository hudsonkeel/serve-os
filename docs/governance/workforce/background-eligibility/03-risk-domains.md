## 1. Purpose

A **Risk Domain** is a category of harm that Serve Caregiving's background eligibility framework exists to guard against. Risk domains are the *why* behind the offense taxonomy: every offense category in [`06-offense-taxonomy.md`](./06-offense-taxonomy.md) exists because it implicates one or more of the risk domains defined here.

Separating risk domains from offense categories matters for two reasons. First, it keeps the reasoning behind the taxonomy visible and defensible — a reviewer or regulator asking "why does this offense disqualify?" gets an answer grounded in a named risk, not a bare list. Second, it gives the framework a stable structure to extend from: new offenses can be evaluated against existing risk domains without having to invent new categories each time.

## 2. The Risk Domains

### 2.1 Violence & Physical Safety

Risk that an individual may cause physical harm to a client, a client's family, a coworker, or the public. This is the most direct threat to the population Serve Caregiving serves — clients are frequently physically frail, and caregiving relationships often occur without a third party present.

### 2.2 Sexual Misconduct & Abuse

Risk of sexual harm, exploitation, or misconduct directed at any person, with particular weight given to findings involving minors or dependent adults. Given the intimacy and privacy of in-home care, this domain receives the framework's strictest treatment.

### 2.3 Abuse, Neglect & Exploitation of Vulnerable Persons

Risk directed specifically at elderly, disabled, or otherwise dependent individuals — Serve Caregiving's client population by definition. This domain exists as its own category, distinct from general violence, because offenses against vulnerable persons are treated as a direct signal of unsuitability for this specific population, regardless of how the offense might be evaluated in a general employment context.

### 2.4 Financial & Property Trust

Risk of theft, fraud, or financial exploitation, particularly where a caregiver has access to a client's home, belongings, financial instruments, or identifying information. Caregivers routinely work unsupervised inside a client's home; this domain reflects the trust that access requires.

### 2.5 Healthcare Fraud & Regulatory Integrity

Risk associated with fraud against healthcare payers or programs, exclusion from federal or state healthcare programs, or other findings that implicate an individual's fitness to participate in a regulated healthcare-adjacent workforce. This domain is closely tied to legal and regulatory exclusion requirements — see §4.

### 2.6 Substance-Related Risk

Risk associated with substance use or distribution that could impair judgment or safety in a caregiving context, distinct from the criminal severity of the underlying offense itself. A single, dated possession offense and an active distribution operation both touch this domain, but at very different levels of concern — which is precisely why offense-level detail, not just domain membership, drives classification (see [`06-offense-taxonomy.md`](./06-offense-taxonomy.md)).

### 2.7 Dishonesty & Integrity

Risk associated with findings that speak to an individual's general honesty and reliability — forgery, embezzlement, identity theft, and similar offenses — independent of whether the dishonesty was directed at a healthcare or caregiving context specifically.

### 2.8 Public Safety & Driving Risk

Risk associated with an individual's fitness to operate a vehicle safely, relevant specifically because many Serve Caregiving roles include transportation duties (see [`04-role-exposure-model.md`](./04-role-exposure-model.md) §2.3). DWI and related offenses sit primarily in this domain, though repeated or aggravated instances may also implicate §2.1.

## 3. How Risk Domains Are Used

Risk domains are a **classification input**, not a classification themselves. An offense may implicate more than one domain (a DWI causing injury touches both §2.1 and §2.8, for example); the offense taxonomy resolves that overlap into a single classification per the rules in [`01-background-eligibility-ontology.md`](./01-background-eligibility-ontology.md) §3.2–3.3. Risk domains are the reasoning layer that makes each entry in the offense taxonomy explainable, not an independent scoring mechanism.

## 4. Legal Note

**Requires Legal Review.** Several risk domains above — particularly Healthcare Fraud & Regulatory Integrity (§2.5) and Abuse/Neglect/Exploitation of Vulnerable Persons (§2.3) — intersect with specific statutory and regulatory screening obligations (including, without limitation, federal and state healthcare exclusion lists, sex offender registries, and state-specific vulnerable-adult or nurse-aide registries). This framework does not currently specify which registries or exclusion databases must be checked, in what circumstances, or how findings from them are incorporated. That determination requires legal review and will be incorporated into a future revision of this module.
